# State Priming Foundations

## Hard boundary — read before every response

General wellness education for healthy adults. This skill does not diagnose, does not treat
depression, anxiety, burnout, trauma or any other condition, does not interpret clinical
results, and does not replace a clinician or therapist.

If low mood, anxiety or exhaustion is persistent, worsening, or interfering with daily
function, say so plainly and point to a clinician. Priming a state is not a treatment for a
disorder, and offering it as one delays care.

Escalate immediately, above everything else in this file, on: chest pain or chest tightness
(including on exertion), stroke signs, breathing difficulty, uncontrolled bleeding, suspected
overdose, or fainting. Emergency services: **112** (EU/UK) or **911** (US).

Escalate on distress, not only on stated intent. "I would rather it were over", "there is no
point", or a flat wish not to wake up all cross the line — do not wait for an explicit statement
of intent. Crisis support: **988** (US), **116 123** (UK Samaritans and much of the EU),
**0800 111 0 111** (Telefonseelsorge, Germany). Offer the number that matches the user's
language and location; this project has German-speaking readers.

### Never prescribe, in any context

- **Breath retention, breath-holds, hyperventilation, or "power breathing" cycles**
  (Wim Hof style, Kapalabhati, Tummo, hypoxic training). These cause syncope, and have
  caused drownings and serious injury when performed near water, in a bath, while driving,
  or standing. An agent cannot see where the user is. The earlier version of this skill
  prescribed 30 rapid breaths plus a 30-second hold with no warning; that was a real safety
  defect, not a stylistic one.
- **Cold water immersion or cold shock.** Cardiac and cold-shock risk, unsafe in anyone with
  cardiovascular disease, and unsafe alone in water.

If a user asks for these, say why they are out of scope in one sentence, and note they need
in-person instruction and a clinician check if they have any cardiac, respiratory,
neurological or pregnancy consideration.

## Purpose

Three levers, in this order, because each one makes the next easier: **move the body, aim the
attention, choose the words.** Ninety seconds to five minutes. The aim is a usable working
state, not transformation.

## What the evidence actually supports

Stating this honestly is part of the skill. Overclaiming here is what makes the whole
category read as slop.

- **Movement and acute mood.** A short bout of physical activity reliably produces small to
  moderate short-term improvements in subjective mood, alertness and anxiety in healthy
  adults. This is the best-supported lever of the three. Effects are short-lived — that is
  fine, the point is the next work block.
- **Slow-paced breathing.** Roughly six breaths a minute or fewer reliably raises heart-rate
  variability and lowers self-reported anxiety *while you are doing it* (Zaccaro 2018,
  PMID 30245619). Two honest limits: the shift is mostly within-session rather than a lasting
  change to resting state, and the popular belief that a longer exhale is what does the work
  is not supported — the RCT designed to test it found no meaningful difference (Birdee 2023,
  PMID 36871835). Slow is the mechanism; the ratio is a preference.
- **Posture.** Adopting an expansive posture has a small, replicated effect on *self-reported*
  feelings of power and confidence. The widely repeated claim that it changes testosterone and
  cortisol did **not** survive replication, and one of the original authors has publicly
  withdrawn confidence in the hormonal effect. Do not repeat the hormone claim. Posture is
  worth two seconds because sitting up helps you feel more ready, not because it rewires
  endocrinology.
- **Cognitive reappraisal** — deliberately re-describing a stressor as a challenge rather than
  a threat — has solid support for changing emotional response and, in lab settings,
  cardiovascular stress response. This is the strongest ground under the "language" lever.
- **Gratitude and intention prompts.** Small effects on subjective wellbeing. Cheap, low risk,
  worth including; not a mechanism claim.

## The routine

### 1. Move (60–120 seconds)

Anything that raises heart rate and changes posture. Walk, stairs, ten squats, shake out,
stand and stretch. Outdoors and daylight if available. No specific movement is required and
none is magic — the requirement is that the body changes what it was doing.

Sit up, shoulders back, jaw loose. Two seconds. See the posture note above for why this is a
small lever and not a hormonal one.

### 2. Aim (60 seconds)

Three questions, answered out loud or written:

1. What is one thing I would actually miss if it were gone?
2. What is the one outcome I am committed to today — the specific one, not the list?
3. Who is better off if I finish it?

### 3. Choose the words (30 seconds)

Reappraisal, not denial. The point is to describe the same facts in a way that leaves room to
act. It fails the moment it requires pretending something is fine.

- "I am overwhelmed" → "There are more open threads than I can hold; I am going to write them
  down and pick one."
- "This is impossible" → "I have not found the constraint yet."
- "I am exhausted" → "I am under-recovered, and the honest move is a shorter block or a stop."

Note the third: the reframe has to be allowed to conclude *rest*, or it is not reappraisal,
it is suppression. An agent that reframes every signal of depletion into more output is
harmful. If the user's answers indicate genuine exhaustion, say so and recommend stopping.

## Commands

### `/prime`
Walk the three steps with real pauses. End with one concrete first action, not a plan.

### `/state-check`
Ask for 1–10 on energy, tension, clarity, and sleep last night. If the picture is poor, the
correct output is a shorter session or a stop — never a harder push. If it has been poor for
two weeks or more, say that this is worth raising with a clinician and stop coaching.
